Overview

The week ends with a leadership shock at Nvidia, the company whose chips run most AI systems, and with a two-day Microsoft 365 outage that is now fixed. OpenAI let its AI agents pay for purchases, Google released a faster everyday model, and a new lawsuit asks a court to stop the five largest AI developers from releasing new models. In Europe, lawmakers are pushing new rules on deepfakes and on manipulative interface design.

Four items are from 11–12 November. They are older than 24 hours but are still the main stories today, so they are included with their real dates.

News

  1. 01Field-wide · big company decisions

    Nvidia's founder steps down as CEO and the finance chief takes over for now

    What happenedNvidia said on 12 November that Jensen Huang has left his roles as president, CEO and board member, with immediate effect. Colette Kress, the chief financial officer, is interim CEO while the board looks for a permanent leader inside and outside the company. News reports link the exit to an investigation into Huang's personal relationships and a possible foreign intelligence connection.

    Why it mattersAlmost all large AI training and cloud AI services depend on Nvidia hardware. A sudden change at the top creates uncertainty about supply, prices and export rules for everyone who builds or buys AI systems.

    Source: Nvidia announcement (SEC filing) and TechCrunch · 12 Nov 2026

  2. 02Information systems

    Microsoft 365 is back after an outage that reached more than 70 countries

    What happenedOutlook, Teams and Copilot stopped working for many users from around midday on Thursday (US time). Microsoft said on Saturday that all services are restored. Governments, hospitals, airlines and banks were affected. Microsoft says the cause was not a cyberattack and not AI agents; it pointed to servers that became unavailable during automated checks.

    Why it mattersThis is a clear teaching case about dependence on one cloud provider. It shows why organisations need continuity plans for email and communication, not only for their own systems.

    Source: NBC News · 14 Nov 2026

  3. 03Artificial intelligence · tools

    OpenAI's agents can now pay: ChatGPT Wallet starts in the United States

    What happenedOpenAI launched ChatGPT Wallet on 13 November for its always-on agents, which it calls "dots". A user saves a card once; the agent prepares a purchase, and the user approves each one with a face or fingerprint check. The agent and the shop never see the real card number. It was built with American Express, Mastercard and Visa and is limited to US subscribers on the Pro plan.

    Why it mattersIt moves agents from giving advice to making transactions. The design question is where the human approval step sits, and web shops must now decide how to recognise real agent traffic instead of blocking it as bots.

    Source: Android Authority and GSMArena · 13 Nov 2026

  4. 04Artificial intelligence · new models

    Google releases Gemini 4 Hydrogen, a faster and cheaper everyday model

    What happenedGoogle released Gemini 4 Hydrogen on 12 November. It replaces Gemini 3.8 Flash for everyday tasks, coding and multi-step agent work, and is open to everyone, including free users of the Gemini app. The price for developers is $1.50 per million input tokens and $7.50 per million output tokens.

    Why it mattersCheaper, faster models make it realistic to put agents inside ordinary applications and search. It also changes cost calculations for student and research projects that use an API.

    Note: the speed figure (2.5 times faster than the older model) and the benchmark comparisons are Google's own claims.

    Source: Google blog · 12 Nov 2026

  5. 05Artificial intelligence · safety and law

    Four advocacy groups ask a California court to stop new AI models

    What happenedOn 12 November, Public Citizen, the Irreplaceable Education Fund, Heat Initiative and the Alliance for Secure AI sued Anthropic, Google, Meta, OpenAI and SpaceXSI in San Francisco. They want a judge to halt the development and release of new models, arguing the companies cannot prevent serious harm. The companies had not commented when the story was published.

    Why it mattersIt tests whether existing consumer and nuisance law can be used to govern AI development when no specific AI law applies. The result could influence how risk and responsibility are discussed in AI ethics courses.

    Note: this is a claim by one side in a new case. No court has ruled on it.

    Source: Associated Press via ABC News · 12 Nov 2026

  6. 06Field-wide · regulation

    White House task force wants softer liability rules so AI firms report incidents

    What happenedAt a meeting on Thursday, officials from the US president's AI task force advised government agencies to relax their cyber liability policies. The idea is that AI companies will share information about serious incidents more openly if reporting carries less legal risk.

    Why it mattersIncident reporting is a core part of information security governance. This is a different approach from the EU, which relies more on legal duties and fines.

    Not confirmed: the report is based on one unnamed official, and the meeting was not public.

    Source: Bloomberg · 14 Nov 2026

  7. 07Information science · privacy

    European Parliament calls for a fundamental right to protect your own likeness from deepfakes

    What happenedEU lawmakers adopted a resolution on Thursday asking for the EU Charter of Fundamental Rights to be updated. They want it to protect a person's identity and likeness against AI deepfakes and to guard against discrimination by algorithms. Today the EU has no single rule for this and uses the AI Act, the Digital Services Act and GDPR together.

    Why it mattersIt treats a person's face and voice as protected information in their own right. A resolution is a political request and does not change the law by itself, but it shows the direction of future rules.

    Source: MLex · 13 Nov 2026

  8. 08Information science · UX

    EU proposes a Digital Fairness Act that bans dark patterns

    What happenedThe European Commission presented the proposal on 11 November. It would ban manipulative interface tricks, with a list of ten prohibited patterns, and require that users can switch off features made to keep them hooked, such as autoplay and endless scrolling. It also demands clear prices and an easy way to cancel subscriptions. The Commission could fine companies up to 6% of worldwide turnover in large cross-border cases.

    Why it mattersThis would turn UX ethics into law. Interface designers would need to show that a design does not push users into choices they did not intend.

    Note: it is only a proposal. The European Parliament and the Council must agree, and the text may change.

    Source: European Commission and Quartz · 11 Nov 2026

  9. 09Field-wide · job market

    Tech layoffs in 2026 pass 206,000, but AI explains only part of them

    What happenedThe tracker TrueUp counted 206,817 affected tech workers in 712 layoff events as of 13 November. That is close to last year's total of about 211,000. Companies often give AI investment as the reason.

    Why it mattersIt affects what students can expect after graduation. It is also a good example of why claims about AI and jobs need careful reading of the data.

    Sources disagree: another tracker gives about 135,000. One review of 119 layoffs labelled as AI-related found that only 27 came with a company statement that AI had really reduced the need for staff.

    Source: TrueUp tracker, Coinpaper · 13 Nov 2026, and BBN Times · 12 Nov 2026

  9. ۰۹کل حوزه · بازار کار
